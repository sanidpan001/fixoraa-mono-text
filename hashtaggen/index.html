What hashtags actually do

A hashtag turns a word into a link. When you add a hashtag to a post, that post joins a public collection of every other post using the same tag, and anyone browsing that collection can discover your post even if they do not follow you. That is the whole mechanism, and it explains both the power and the limits of hashtags. They help discovery, but they do not create good content: a weak post with thirty hashtags still performs like a weak post, while a strong post with five relevant hashtags can travel far, because people who find it through a tag are already interested in the topic.

Instagram, TikTok, YouTube and X all use hashtags, but they use them differently. On Instagram, hashtags are a discovery tool for reels and posts, and they work best when they are specific to the post rather than generic. On TikTok, hashtags help the recommendation system understand a video's topic, but trending sounds and watch time matter more. On YouTube, hashtags in a title or description create clickable links above the title, but only the first three are shown prominently. The advice below focuses on Instagram, where hashtag strategy is most developed, and most of it transfers to other platforms with small adjustments.

How many hashtags should you use

Instagram allows up to thirty hashtags per post, but using all thirty is rarely the best choice. Most creators find that ten to fifteen well-chosen hashtags outperform thirty generic ones, because relevance beats volume. A set that mixes sizes works best: a few large tags with millions of posts for broad reach, several medium tags with tens of thousands of posts where your post has a realistic chance of appearing near the top, and a few small, niche tags with a few thousand posts where your post can stay visible for days. A post that only uses huge tags disappears in minutes under a flood of new posts, while a post that only uses tiny tags reaches almost nobody new.

Reels follow the same logic with one extra rule: keep the caption readable. Hashtags can go in the caption or in the first comment, and both work for discovery, so put three to five in the caption if you want them visible, and the rest in a comment if you prefer a clean caption. For stories, hashtags work differently again: a story hashtag sticker links the story to a tag page, but more than one or two stickers clutters the story, so pick the single most relevant tag.

Hashtag sets by topic

Fitness posts do well with a mix of training tags and community tags: tags about the workout itself, such as strength training or home workouts, tags about the goal, such as fitness journey or healthy lifestyle, and tags about the community, such as gym community or fit family. Food posts should name the dish, the cuisine, and the occasion: a pasta post wants pasta lover and Italian food, but also home cooking and dinner ideas, because people browse by meal as well as by ingredient. Travel posts should name the place, the type of trip, and the feeling: a mountain post wants mountain views and hiking adventures, but also wanderlust and travel diary, because the audience for a place and the audience for a mood overlap but are not identical.

Small business posts need a different mix again. Half the set should describe the product or service, and half should describe the customer or location: a bakery in a city wants both artisan bread and the city name, because local customers search locally. Add one branded tag — your business name as a hashtag — to every post, and encourage customers to use it, so that over months you build a collection of customer photos under your own tag. That collection is more valuable than any single viral post, because it is social proof that belongs to you.

Reels hashtags: what changes

Reels are discovered mainly through the recommendation feed, not through hashtag pages, so hashtags matter less for reels than for grid posts, but they still help the system classify your video. Use five to ten tags for a reel, lead with the most specific tag, and include one tag that describes the format — such as reels, tutorial, or behind the scenes — because format tags help the system match your reel to viewers who like that format. Avoid trending tags that have nothing to do with your video: they may bring a brief spike of views from the wrong audience, but those viewers will swipe away immediately, and that low watch time hurts the reel more than the extra views help it.

Mistakes that hurt reach

  • Copying the same thirty hashtags onto every post, which looks automated and teaches the system nothing new.
  • Using banned or broken tags, which can hide a post from tag pages entirely.
  • Adding trending tags unrelated to the post, which brings the wrong audience and low watch time.
  • Using only huge tags, where a post disappears under new posts within minutes.
  • Stuffing hashtags into the middle of a caption, which breaks readability for no benefit.
  • Never checking which tags actually brought views, so the same weak set is reused forever.

Check your insights after a few posts: Instagram shows which views came from hashtags, and that number tells you whether your set is working. Rotate one or two tags per post, keep the ones that bring views, drop the ones that never do, and build a small library of proven sets for your recurring topics. Building a hashtag library over time

The most efficient hashtag habit is building a small library instead of inventing a set from scratch for every post. Start with three or four sets for your recurring topics — one for product posts, one for behind-the-scenes posts, one for customer stories, and one for seasonal posts — and save each set in a notes app where you can copy it in seconds. Each time you post, pick the closest set, swap two or three tags to fit the specific post, and paste it. This keeps your hashtags relevant without eating into the time you should spend creating the post itself.

Review the library monthly using your post insights. Note which sets brought hashtag views and which brought almost none, and retire the weakest tags rather than carrying them out of habit. Over a few months, this review process turns a generic library into a personal one: tags that work for your audience, your niche and your location, rather than tags copied from a popular account whose audience is completely different from yours. That personal library is the real asset, and it is worth far more than any single list of trending tags.

Frequently asked questions

How many hashtags should I use on Instagram?

Ten to fifteen relevant hashtags usually outperform thirty generic ones. Mix large, medium and small tags so your post gets both reach and lasting visibility.

Do hashtags work on reels?

Yes, but less than on grid posts. Use five to ten specific tags to help the system classify your reel, and focus more on watch time and the hook.

Where should I put hashtags?

In the caption or the first comment — both work for discovery. Put a few in the caption for visibility and the rest in a comment for a clean look.

What are niche hashtags?

Small tags with under fifty thousand posts, focused on a specific topic or place. Your post stays visible there longer and reaches people who care about that exact topic.

Should I use the same hashtags every time?

No. Reusing the identical set looks automated and teaches the system nothing new. Rotate a few tags per post and keep the ones that bring views.

What is a branded hashtag?

A tag using your business or creator name, added to every post, which collects your community's posts in one place over time.

Can hashtags get my post hidden?

Yes, if you use banned or broken tags, or unrelated trending tags. Check a tag page before using a new tag, and avoid tags that show no recent posts.

Do hashtags work on TikTok and YouTube?

Yes, with differences. TikTok uses tags to classify videos for recommendations, and YouTube shows only the first three hashtags prominently above a title.
