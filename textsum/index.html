How to Summarize Well: What a Good Summary Keeps, Cuts and Clarifies

A good summary is not a shorter version of a text; it is a clearer version of its argument. It keeps the main ideas, drops the examples and repetitions, and preserves the order of the original reasoning so that a reader who has never seen the full text can still understand what it claimed and why. Learning to summarise is therefore learning to read for structure, not just for details, and it is one of the most useful skills in school, work and everyday life.

The first step in summarising is to find the main idea of each paragraph. Most paragraphs have a topic sentence — often the first sentence — that states the point, followed by evidence, examples or explanation. A summary keeps the topic sentences and usually drops the supporting detail, unless a detail is itself the point. If you cannot state a paragraph's main idea in one sentence, you have not yet understood it well enough to summarise it.

Extractive summarising, the method TextSum uses, works by selecting the most important sentences from the original rather than rewriting them. It scores each sentence by how often its key words appear in the whole text, because words that recur are usually central to the topic. Sentences with many high-frequency content words tend to carry the main ideas, while sentences with rare words often carry examples or asides. The method is simple, transparent and surprisingly effective for factual text.

That method has limits, and knowing them makes you a better user. Extractive summaries preserve the original wording, so they cannot simplify complex language or combine two ideas into one sentence. They also work best on well-structured factual writing — articles, reports and essays — and less well on stories, jokes or highly figurative text, where meaning lives between the lines. A summary is a starting point for understanding, not a replacement for reading when precision matters.

Length is a real choice, not a technical detail. A two-sentence summary answers the question, what is this about, in the briefest possible form. A five-sentence summary can preserve the argument's steps: the problem, the evidence and the conclusion. Choosing the length should depend on your purpose. For a quick skim, short is better. For revision before an exam, a longer summary that preserves the reasoning is usually more useful.

Word counts and reading times matter because they help you plan. Knowing that an article is two thousand words — about ten minutes of reading — lets you decide whether to read it now, summarise it first, or save it for later. TextSum shows these stats alongside the summary so that the summary is not just an output but a reading aid: you can see how much you saved and whether the summary captured a fair share of the original's length.

Students use summarising in several distinct ways. Before reading, a summary gives an overview that makes the full text easier to follow. After reading, writing or checking a summary tests whether the main ideas were understood. Before an exam, summaries become revision notes that are far more useful than highlighted pages, because they force the reader to decide what mattered. Each use benefits from a different summary length, which is why the length control matters.

Professionals use summaries differently. A manager skimming reports wants the decision and the reason, not the background. A researcher scanning papers wants the method and the finding. A journalist checking a source wants the claim and the evidence. In each case, a good summary is judged by whether it lets the reader act — decide, cite or follow up — without reading the whole text. If it does, it has done its job, even if it omits interesting details.

There are a few habits that make summaries better, whether human or machine-made. Keep the original order, because reordering ideas can distort cause and effect. Keep numbers, names and key terms exact, because paraphrasing them can introduce errors. And always check the summary against the original's conclusion: if the summary's ending does not match the original's ending, something important was lost or misread along the way.

Finally, remember that summarising is a skill that improves with practice. Try summarising a short article by hand, then compare your version with TextSum's extractive summary. You will often find that you chose similar sentences, which is reassuring, and sometimes you will find that you chose a sentence the machine missed, because you understood a nuance it could not. That comparison is itself a lesson in reading — and in the limits of automation.

Use TextSum as a reading companion, not a reading replacement. Paste a long text, get its shape in seconds, then read the parts that matter most with your full attention. The summary saves you time, but the understanding still belongs to you — and that is exactly how a good tool should work.

Human summarising differs from extractive summarising in an important way: humans can paraphrase, combining several sentences into one and simplifying difficult language. That ability makes human summaries shorter and clearer than extractive ones, but it also introduces risk, because paraphrasing can accidentally change meaning. Extractive summaries avoid that risk by preserving original wording, which is why they are preferred when accuracy matters more than elegance, such as in legal or scientific contexts.

Abstractive summarising, where a system writes new sentences, is the other major approach, and modern language models use it extensively. Abstractive summaries read more naturally and can be much shorter, but they can also hallucinate details that were not in the original. Understanding the difference between extractive and abstractive methods helps you choose the right tool: use extractive when you need fidelity, abstractive when you need fluency, and always verify important claims against the source.

Summarising also has an ethical dimension. A summary that omits a crucial qualification can mislead readers, even if every sentence in it is true. For example, summarising a study's finding without its limitations gives a false impression of certainty. Good summarising therefore preserves not just the main claims but also their scope and uncertainty, and readers should be wary of summaries that sound too confident.

In education, summarising is often taught alongside note-taking, because the two skills reinforce each other. Notes capture details for later use, while summaries capture structure for understanding. A student who can do both — detailed notes and a clear summary — has a complete study system: the summary for revision, the notes for depth. TextSum can support that system by providing a first-draft summary that the student then refines by hand.

Finally, the best test of a summary is whether it helps someone who has not read the original. Give your summary to a friend, ask them to explain the original's main idea back to you, and see what they got right and what they missed. That test reveals both the summary's strengths and its gaps, and it is the same test that professional editors apply. A summary that passes it has done its job, however it was made.

A practical exercise is to summarise a news article in three sentences by hand, then compare your version with TextSum's output. Where they agree, you have confirmed the main ideas. Where they differ, ask which version a reader would find clearer, and why. That comparison trains judgement about what matters in a text, which is the core skill behind all summarising.

Another exercise is to summarise the same text at two lengths — two sentences and five sentences — and to notice what the longer version adds. Usually it adds the reasoning: the evidence, the qualification or the consequence. Noticing that addition teaches you when a short summary is enough and when a longer one is necessary, which is a decision readers and writers make every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does TextSum choose sentences?

It scores each sentence by how often its important words appear in the whole text, then picks the highest-scoring sentences in their original order.

Is the summary a rewrite?

No. TextSum is extractive: it selects original sentences rather than rewriting them, so wording, names and numbers stay exact.

What kinds of text work best?

Factual articles, essays and reports work best. Stories, jokes and highly figurative writing are harder to summarise by extraction.

How long should my summary be?

Choose short for a quick overview and long for revision or research, where preserving the argument's steps matters more.
