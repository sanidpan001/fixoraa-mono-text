How to use it

First, run one of the presets to see the shape of a realistic answer. Then replace the preset values with your own, changing one input at a time and watching how the result moves — that sensitivity tells you which measurement deserves the most care. Finally, copy the result you settle on so everyone involved works from the same numbers instead of from separate memories of the same conversation.

If a result surprises you, resist the urge to double every input in frustration. Check the inputs first, then change one at a time and watch the result move, and compare the outcome with a real example from your own past experience. That comparison usually reveals whether the calculator or your memory is closer to reality, and the corrected inputs become experience you can reuse next time.

Accuracy and limits

The result is as accurate as the inputs you enter, and the method is exact for the model it uses — but every model simplifies reality a little. Real rooms are rarely perfect rectangles, real groups rarely behave exactly as planned, and real materials vary from batch to batch. Read the result alongside those realities, add a sensible margin where the cost of being short is high, and re-run the calculation once real measurements or confirmations arrive. Used that way, the tool stays honest: a guide for decisions, not a substitute for looking at the real thing.

Rounding deserves a word of its own, because it is where small tools most often mislead. The tool shows results rounded to a readable number of places, which is right for planning but means the last digit is approximate — so do not carry a rounded result into a second calculation and expect exactness at the end. Keep one extra digit while you work through stages, round only the final figure, and remember that a result shown to two decimal places is not a promise of accuracy to two decimal places: it is a readable presentation of an estimate whose true precision depends on how carefully you measured the inputs.
