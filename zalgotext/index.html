What Is Zalgo Text?

Zalgo text is what happens when typography has a bad dream. Normal characters get buried under stacks of combining diacritical marks — the tiny accents, tildes, and hooks that Unicode lets you pile onto any letter — until the text looks like it is glitching, melting, or being consumed by some digital entity. A simple 'hello' becomes a towering stack of corrupted glyphs that spills into the lines above and below.

The name comes from Zalgo, a creepypasta meme from the late 2000s: an eldritch horror said to corrupt comics, text, and eventually reality itself, always announced with the phrase 'HE COMES.' Forum users began rendering Zalgo's speech in corrupted text, and the aesthetic escaped into the wider internet. Today zalgo text is the visual language of cursed memes, horror ARGs, Halloween posts, and anyone who wants their username to look possessed.

How It Works: The Unicode Trick

Unicode — the standard behind all modern text — includes hundreds of combining characters in the range U+0300–U+036F. These are marks designed to sit on top of, below, or through a base letter: accents like é, tildes like ñ, and dozens of more exotic hooks, rings, and strokes. Crucially, Unicode places no limit on how many combining marks can attach to a single character. Your text renderer is obligated to try to stack them all.

Zalgo generators exploit this mercilessly. For each letter of your input, the generator attaches a chosen number of random combining marks — some above (U+0300–U+0314: acute, grave, circumflex, tilde, macron, and friends), some through the middle (U+0315–U+0338), and some below (U+0334–U+0363). The intensity setting in this tool controls exactly how many marks per character: intensity 1 ('mini') adds a subtle shimmer of corruption, intensity 3 ('normal') gives the classic cursed look, and intensity 8+ ('max') produces the full reality-breaking tower. The count is deterministic — intensity × text length marks total — so the same input and intensity always produce the same corruption.

Why It Renders Differently Everywhere

Zalgo text is a stress test for text rendering, and renderers respond differently. Browsers stack the marks vertically using font metrics; some fonts handle 20 stacked marks gracefully, others give up and draw tofu boxes. Line height explodes because the stacked marks overflow the line box — this is why zalgo text famously 'eats' the lines above and below it in comment sections. Mobile renderers are often stricter: iOS historically clipped excessive combining marks, while Android stacked them enthusiastically. Copy-paste usually preserves the marks (they are real characters), but some platforms sanitize them — Discord, for instance, has at various times stripped or limited zalgo in usernames precisely because of the chaos it caused in chat layouts.

A Brief History of Cursed Text

The raw material is older than the meme. Combining characters existed for Vietnamese, polytonic Greek, and phonetic transcription long before anyone thought to abuse them. The abuse began around 2004–2006 on the Something Awful forums, where Zalgo comics — innocent Garfield and Dilbert strips redrawn with cosmic horror — spread with corrupted captions. The 'HE COMES' copypasta ('To invoke the hive-mind representing chaos...') became the canonical Zalgo text, and generators followed within a couple of years.

From there it colonized everything: early Facebook display names, Xbox gamertags (until Microsoft banned them), Minecraft signs, YouTube comments, and the 'cursed' aesthetic of late-2010s meme culture. Each platform's attempt to contain it — character limits on combining marks, username sanitizers — just proved how disruptive a few hundred bytes of Unicode could be to billion-dollar rendering pipelines.

Zalgo vs. Its Cousins

  • Vaporwave / fullwidth text (ＡＥＳＴＨＥＴＩＣ): uses fullwidth Unicode forms for a clean retro look — the aesthetic opposite of zalgo's chaos. (Try our VaporText tool for that.)
  • Upside-down text (ʇxǝʇ): flips characters using lookalike glyphs. Often combined with zalgo for maximum damage.
  • Spoiler / strikethrough: single combining marks (like U+0336, the long stroke overlay) used decoratively — zalgo's polite cousin.
  • 'Aesthetic' spacing (h e l l o): wide letter-spacing for a soft, dreamy feel — zalgo's mirror universe.

The Etiquette of Corruption

Zalgo text is the typographic equivalent of shouting — powerful in small doses, exhausting everywhere. A few community norms have emerged: never put it in usernames or display names on shared platforms (it breaks layouts for everyone, including screen-reader users, for whom stacked marks are read as gibberish or skipped entirely — an accessibility disaster). Keep it to your own posts, memes, and creative projects. Use mini intensity in mixed company; save max for Halloween. And remember that some platforms will strip or mangle it — always check the safe preview and test-paste before announcing HE COMES to your audience.

Accessibility Note

Screen readers handle zalgo text poorly: most announce the base letters and either skip the combining marks or read them as a stream of 'combining acute accent' noise. If your audience includes screen-reader users — and it does — treat zalgo as a purely visual effect, never as the sole carrier of meaning. Put the plain version nearby, or keep the corruption decorative around readable text. Cursed for the eyes should not mean invisible to the ears.

Zalgo in Gaming, Usernames, and Chat Chaos

Zalgo text found its natural habitat in multiplayer games and chat platforms, where a corrupted username renders inside everyone else's interface. Early Xbox Live and PlayStation Network gamertags, Minecraft server signs, and MMO character names all went through a zalgo phase — until platform after platform added sanitizers specifically to strip combining marks from display names. The arms race was instructive: each new filter (limit marks per character, strip non-spacing marks entirely, normalize to NFC form) was a tacit admission that a few bytes of legal Unicode could defeat million-dollar UI layouts. Discord's username system, Minecraft's chat filter, and most modern games now cap or strip zalgo — which is why the generator's safe preview matters: what works in your notes app may vanish in the game client.

Speedrunners and ARG communities still use it deliberately. Horror game ARGs hide corrupted text in fake terminal outputs and 'possessed' NPC dialogue; streamers trigger zalgo alerts for jump scares. In these contexts the rendering chaos is the point — the text is supposed to look like the system is breaking.

Making Cursed Text by Hand

You can corrupt text manually once you know the trick. On most systems, combining marks live in the Unicode range U+0300–U+036F: open a character map, pick marks like U+0301 (combining acute), U+0303 (tilde), U+0313 (comma above), and U+034F, and paste several after any letter. Each additional mark stacks higher or lower. Ten marks per character gives the classic look; thirty gives the reality-breaking tower. The generator above just automates this with a seeded randomizer so the same input always yields the same corruption — handy when you need a consistent 'voice' for a character or a repeatable test string for QA. For quick manual work, remember the three zones: marks U+0300–U+0314 climb upward, U+0334–U+0363 dangle below, and mixing both with a few midline strikethroughs (U+0336) gives the fullest corruption per character.

Zalgo as a QA and Security Test Input

Beyond memes, zalgo strings belong in every test suite that handles user text. Paste max-intensity corruption into usernames, comments, search boxes, and bios, and watch for: layout breakage (line boxes exploding), database truncation (some byte-length limits count marks as characters), and rendering hangs (a few pathological fonts choke on deep stacks). Security teams use it in fuzzing because combining marks have historically triggered bugs in text shaping engines — the same code path that draws your UI. If your app survives zalgo, it survives nearly anything users paste. Keep a max-intensity sample in your test fixtures; future you will be grateful.

Frequently asked questions

What is zalgo text and how does it work?

Zalgo text is normal text with many Unicode combining marks (accents and symbols from U+0300–U+036F) stacked on each letter. Unicode allows unlimited combining marks per character, so generators pile them on until the text looks glitched or corrupted.

The name comes from Zalgo, a creepypasta horror meme from the late 2000s about an entity that corrupts comics and text, always announced with 'HE COMES.' Fans rendered its speech in corrupted text and the style spread across the internet.

Usually yes — the marks are real Unicode characters, so copy-paste preserves them. But some platforms (Discord usernames, Xbox gamertags) sanitize or limit combining marks, and rendering varies between browsers and phones.

What does invert mode do?

Is zalgo text accessible to screen readers?

Poorly — most screen readers skip the combining marks or read them as noise, so zalgo should never be the only carrier of meaning. Keep it decorative, keep the plain text nearby, and avoid it in usernames and shared interfaces.
